# Steal and Deal — story notes

Not in the game yet. This is the shape of it, written down so the systems can
be built with somewhere to put it. Nothing here should go in before **heat**,
**orders** and **territory** exist, because all three are what the story hangs
on — without them it is cutscenes bolted to a sandbox.

---

## The premise

You inherit your brother's chop shop. He died on a job that went wrong. You
pick it up because it is the only thing you know how to do, and because you
want to know who was there when it happened.

The revenge is the reason you keep going. It is not the reason you start. You
start because there is a debt and a garage and nothing else.

---

## What the premise is missing on its own

Two things: **pressure** — a reason to work tonight rather than eventually —
and **complication** — a reason the revenge is not simply a matter of finding
someone and shooting them.

Everything below exists to supply one or the other.

---

## 0. The first day — before any of it

**The game opens with the brother alive**, and it opens boring on purpose.

You drive your own rusty pickup in to work, same as every other morning. He is
already there. You say hello. He tells you what is on for the day, and the
day's work *is* the tutorial:

1. There is a beige beater parked over on the east side. Go and get it.
2. Bring it back to the shop and put it on the ramp.
3. He wants a door, a seat and the hood off it.

That is it. No timer, no police worth worrying about, no debt yet. It teaches
jimmying, hotwiring, driving, the ramp, and three different teardown minigames
without ever calling any of it a tutorial, because it is just Tuesday.

**Why it opens this way:**

- The loop is taught in the safest possible version of itself. Every mechanic
  the rest of the game leans on gets used once, with somebody explaining it in
  the voice of a man who has explained it a hundred times.
- It gives the player a person to lose. A death in a cutscene before the title
  card is an inciting incident; a death after ninety minutes of him telling you
  you are doing it wrong is a bereavement.
- It establishes the shop as a *working business* rather than a hideout. The
  reason you keep it going later is that you have seen it running properly.

**The beater is a specific car in a specific place**, not one of the ambient
spawns. It is the first instance of the wider rule below: story cars are placed,
not generated. See `features.md` — "Placed cars and gated spawns".

**The turn:** he goes out on a job you are not on. He does not come back.
Everything from section 1 onward starts the next morning, in a shop that is
suddenly yours and a diary full of his appointments.

---

## 1. The debt is the tutorial

He did not just die. He died owing somebody.

That somebody turns up in the first week, is perfectly civil about it, and sets
a number and a date. The countdown teaches the loop: steal a car, strip it,
sell the parts, make the payment. No tutorial box, no arrows — the pressure
explains the game.

**Hooks:** existing money, the day/night clock, the buyer list.
**Beat:** first payment made. Relief, then the next number, which is larger.

---

## 2. The shop is half-lost already

He had sold the lift, or borrowed against the building, or took a partner who
owns forty percent and does not intend to sell it back cheaply.

You do not start with the garage. You start with a claim on it, and the
upgrade tree becomes the plot: every tier you buy is a piece of your own shop
bought back off somebody.

**Hooks:** garage tier, the upgrade list.
**Beat:** the deed, whole, in your name. Roughly the midpoint.

---

## 3. The job that killed him is a car, not a person

Whatever he was doing that night, there was a specific car involved, and it is
still out there with somebody driving it.

Finding it is the spine. Every district you get access to is a lead, not a
level. Somebody saw it. Somebody sold it a set of plates. Somebody resprayed it
— badly.

**The choice at the end of the thread:** get it on your ramp and you can strip
it, which destroys the only evidence and makes you safe; or keep it whole and
keep the thing that can be traced back to the people who were there. Safety or
leverage, and you cannot have both.

**Hooks:** car heat, the respray/sander laundering systems, districts.

---

## 4. The person responsible is also the only good fence

The one who set him up pays better than anyone else in the city and asks the
fewest questions.

You need them alive and doing business with you for most of the game. Every
payment you take off them funds the thing you intend to do to them, and every
one makes it harder — because you are now somebody they know, somebody they
trust, somebody who has been to their house.

**Hooks:** buyer list, gang standing.
**Beat:** the moment you find out. Ideally mid-game, with a lot of money still
to be made off them.

---

## 5. The brother was not clean

The further in you get, the clearer it becomes that he was into worse than
cars. The debt was not from a bad month.

"Picking up the pieces" starts to mean inheriting things you did not sign up
for: people who assume you know what he knew, jobs that were promised in your
name, a reputation you did not earn and cannot correct.

**The late question:** are you avenging him or finishing what he started, and
at what point did those stop being different things.

---

## Shape

- **Act 0 — the ordinary day.** He is alive. You do three jobs for him. The
  game teaches itself. Ends with him not coming home.
- **Act 1 — the debt.** Learn the loop under a deadline. Small cars, one
  district, one buyer. Ends with the first payment and the realisation that
  the debt does not end.
- **Act 2 — the shop.** Buy the garage back piece by piece. Territory opens
  up, the fence relationship deepens, the car surfaces as a rumour.
- **Act 3 — the car.** Track it down through the districts. Find out who the
  fence is. Choose what the car is for.

---

## Tone

Dry, unglamorous, close to the ground. Nobody in this world is a criminal
mastermind; they are people with tools and debts. The comedy is in how mundane
the crime is — the paperwork of it, the waiting, the fact that the hardest part
of stealing a car is finding somewhere to put it.

Keep the writing in the same register as the in-game text: plain, specific,
slightly weary, and never impressed with itself.
